Why This Exists

Air pollution is one of the most significant and underreported environmental health risks in Southeast Asia. In Siem Reap, Cambodia, fine particulate matter from vehicle exhaust, biomass burning, and seasonal agricultural fires regularly pushes air quality into ranges that carry serious health consequences — yet publicly available, hyperlocal data is virtually non-existent.

This project exists to change that, one sensor at a time. By placing a research-grade particle counter outdoors and sharing its readings openly on the web, I give residents, visitors, and researchers a reliable, real-time picture of the air they are breathing. No paywalls, no accounts, no lag — just accurate local data, continuously updated.

The goal is simple: make the invisible visible. Whether you are a parent deciding whether to let your children play outside, a cyclist planning a morning route, or a public-health researcher studying regional air quality trends, this dashboard is built for you.

02

How It Works

Every number on this dashboard traces a clear, auditable path from physical sensor to your screen. Here is how the data is collected, corrected, stored, and visualised.

  • Sensor A Sensirion SEN55 environmental node measures PM1.0, PM2.5, PM4.0, PM10, temperature, and relative humidity using laser-scattering optical particle counting. The SEN55 is a calibrated, industrial-grade sensor used in academic and commercial air-quality networks worldwide.
  • Hardware The sensor is wired to an ESP32-C3 Mini microcontroller running ESPHome firmware. ESPHome reads the SEN55 over I²C every 60 seconds and publishes the measurements over Wi-Fi to a local Home Assistant instance.
  • AQI Calculation The Air Quality Index is derived from the PM2.5 reading using the US EPA NowCast formula. A humidity correction factor is applied before this calculation because optical counters overestimate particle concentrations in high-humidity conditions — a common occurrence in Cambodia's tropical climate.
  • Storage All sensor readings flow from Home Assistant into a local InfluxDB time-series database. InfluxDB retains the full history, enabling efficient range queries for 24-hour charts, 7-day trend summaries, and year-long heatmaps without degrading the live API response time.
  • Visualisation A lightweight Flask backend queries InfluxDB and serves a JSON API. The frontend is plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript — no heavy frameworks — with charts rendered by Chart.js. The live dashboard refreshes automatically every 60 seconds.
  • Open Source The complete source code — sensor firmware, backend, and frontend — is published on GitHub. Anyone can inspect the calculations, fork the project, or deploy their own monitoring station.
03

The Build

The outdoor sensor unit is built from off-the-shelf components and a 3D-printed enclosure. At its core is a Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32-C3 wired to a Sensirion SEN55 over I²C, running ESPHome firmware and reporting to Home Assistant over Wi-Fi every 60 seconds.

  • Microcontroller Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32-C3
  • Sensor Sensirion SEN55-SDN-T — PM1.0, PM2.5, PM4.0, PM10, temperature, humidity
  • Enclosure 3D-printed SEN55 case by Syvel Engineering
  • Antenna External 2.4GHz Wi-Fi antenna via U.FL connector

The enclosure STL files were designed by Syvel Engineering and printed by 3D Print Cambodia in Phnom Penh, who were able to ship to Siem Reap at a very reasonable price.

The case was originally designed for an ESP8266 dev board, so the XIAO ESP32-C3 — being much smaller — needed a small dab of hot glue to stay in place. The SEN55 fits snugly in the left chamber, with the ESP32-C3 and antenna cable routed into the right side. A hole was punched through the top of the case using the awl on a Swiss Army knife to pass the antenna cable through. The finished unit is mounted to the wall outdoors with blue sticky tack.
